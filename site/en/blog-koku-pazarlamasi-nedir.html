What is scent marketing? What research says about ambient scent

Scent marketing (ambient scenting) aims to shape how visitors perceive a space by giving it a deliberately chosen scent. Hotel lobbies, shops, showrooms and offices use it most. So what does scientific research say about the effect, and how should the findings be read?

Three key studies

1. Store evaluations and time perception (1996)

The study by Spangenberg, Crowley and Henderson in the Journal of Marketing was one of the first thorough experiments on the effect of ambient scent in retail. Where a pleasant ambient scent was present, participants rated the store and its products more favourably and were more likely to say they would return. A widely quoted finding concerns time perception: in the scented setting, participants felt they had spent less time in the store than they actually had.

2. Brand recall (2003)

In two experiments reported in the Journal of Marketing Research, Morrin and Ratneshwar found that ambient scent improved recall and recognition of both familiar and unfamiliar brands. Two details stand out: the effect did not depend on whether the scent matched the product category, and participants' self-reported mood did not change. According to the researchers, attention is the most likely explanation: in the scented setting participants looked at the products for longer.

3. A meta-analysis of thirty years (2017)

In the Journal of Retailing, Roschk, Loureiro and Breitsohl combined 66 studies (135 effects, 15,621 participants) on the effects of music, scent and colour. Scented settings scored higher than unscented ones on pleasure, satisfaction and behavioural intention. The size of the effect, however, was small to medium. The data also suggest that scent tends to have a stronger effect in service settings (such as hotels and restaurants) than in retail.

Reading the findings correctly

Taken together, these studies paint a clear picture: a well-chosen ambient scent can measurably improve how a space is perceived. Three points are worth keeping in mind:

  • The effect is consistent, not large. The meta-analysis finds a small to medium effect. Be sceptical of firm promises such as "increases sales by X per cent"; any such result varies greatly with the space, the product and how it is measured.
  • The scent must be pleasant. The positive results came from scents participants found pleasant. A scent that bothers people or is too strong can have the opposite effect.
  • Intensity matters. People quickly get used to a scent they are constantly exposed to. The right intensity and timing keep the scent noticeable without being intrusive. See our article on scent fatigue for details.

Four steps in practice

  1. Start with the identity of the space. A luxury hotel lobby, a gym and a law firm should not smell the same. The scent family (fresh, floral, woody) should match the tone of the space.
  2. Choose the device by volume. The device's coverage should meet the air volume of the space. You can start with the volume calculator.
  3. Be consistent. For businesses with several branches, using the same scent at the same intensity everywhere makes it easier to associate the scent with the brand.
  4. Collect feedback. In the first weeks, ask staff and guests about the intensity and adjust the device's spray and pause times accordingly.

Scent fatigue: how to set the intensity of a space's scent

The scent you notice when you walk into a space seems to have almost disappeared a few minutes later. That does not mean the device has stopped; your nose has got used to it. The most common mistake in space scenting is turning up the intensity to make up for this.

What is habituation?

According to the 2017 review by Pellegrino, Sinding, de Wijk and Hummel, olfactory habituation is the decrease in behavioural response with repeated or continuous exposure; adaptation refers to the neural processes behind this decrease. The review notes that habituation to odours happens relatively quickly, and even faster in higher brain processes than in the receptors in the nose.

The practical consequence: staff working in a space all day barely notice the scent, while a newly arrived guest notices it clearly. Intensity should be set for the visitor's perception, not the staff's.

Tips for the right intensity

  • Start low. Keep the first setting low and increase it gradually over a few days if needed.
  • Run it in intervals. Professional diffusers let you set spray and pause times separately. Short spray, long pause cycles instead of continuous spraying reduce both habituation and fragrance use.
  • Schedule it by hours. With app or timer models the device runs only during opening hours, and intensity can be raised at busy times.
  • Do the "fresh nose" test. Ask someone who has not been in the space for a while: if they notice it at the entrance but it does not bother them, you are at the right level.

Indoor air quality

Fragrance products contain volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that evaporate easily at room temperature. The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) lists air fresheners among indoor VOC sources and notes that concentrations of many VOCs are consistently higher indoors than outdoors (up to ten times higher). The EPA's advice is simple: follow the product's directions for use and increase ventilation when using products that emit VOCs.

In space scenting this means:

  • Do not use more scent than needed; the aim is to leave a noticeable trace, not to fill the air.
  • Make sure the space's ventilation is working.
  • Choose a low intensity in small, closed and stuffy rooms.
  • Ask your supplier for the fragrance's safety data sheet (SDS) and compliance documents. See our article on IFRA.

Sources

  1. Pellegrino, R., Sinding, C., de Wijk, R. A. and Hummel, T. (2017). Habituation and adaptation to odors in humans. Physiology & Behavior, 177, 13-19.
  2. U.S. EPA. Volatile Organic Compounds' Impact on Indoor Air Quality.
